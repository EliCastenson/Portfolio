Custom RC car

About a year ago I set out to build an RC car from scratch, with the idea of starting a Formula RC league in my engineering club. After a lot of redesigns, it finally drove for the first time, and it's the project that taught me the most.

1Research

What's inside an RC car

I started by researching what most RC cars have. My dad even showed me pictures of his own RC car from the 1980s. Every one I looked at came down to the same core parts:

Radio receiverPicks up the signals from the handheld transmitter
BLDC motorDrives the wheels
ESCSends power to each of the motor's phases
Steering servoTurns the front wheels
BatteryPowers everything

I ordered one of each from Amazon, picking whatever looked like the best value. More on that later.

2Rethinking the CAD

Version 1 against version 2

My first design was hard to change and hard to print. The second time, I changed how I designed, not just what I designed.

First attempt

  • Every part modeled in one file, so moving one thing meant moving everything
  • Joints that needed extreme accuracy from the printer
  • No fasteners: everything printed to fit in place
  • Result: prints rarely worked

Current design

  • Each part in its own file
  • Designs that tolerate normal print accuracy
  • Heat-set inserts and screws to fasten parts together
  • Result: parts fit and can be swapped out

From my notebook

Before rebuilding, I wrote down everything wrong with version 1, worked out dimensions by hand, sketched individual parts, and planned the hardware so the new parts could be fastened instead of press-fit.

3Suspension and steering

Getting the wheels right

The suspension arms came out too short

Problem
The suspension arms were way shorter than they needed to be.
Why
I used a radius where I meant a diameter.
Fix
Designed a new part that fit the existing arms and fastened it on.

So much positive camber the car couldn't move

Problem
The wheels tilted out at the top so far that the car couldn't roll.
Why
The pull arms that support the top of each wheel set the camber, and my first lengths were off.
Fix
Kept redesigning and testing pull arm lengths until all four wheels sat at slightly negative camber.

What I'm proud of: the bent-rod steering linkage

I bent M2 stainless steel rods by hand to make the steering linkage. After some practice I could bend them precisely enough that the toe on both front wheels came out almost identical.

4The drivetrain

One problem led to the next

The drivetrain is where most of my time went. Each fix exposed a new problem, mostly because the parts I'd ordered were never chosen to work together.

  1. Printed my own differential

    I tried designing a 3D printed differential for the rear wheels, but I couldn't get the dimensional accuracy I needed on the small gears, and they wouldn't carry load. I switched to an off-the-shelf differential.

  2. The PETG-CF bevel gear shredded instantly

    I designed a PETG-CF bevel gear to drive the differential. The motor I'd ordered was powerful enough that it shredded the gear the first time I tried to drive the car.

  3. The metal gear didn't fit the motor

    I ordered the matching metal bevel gear, but never checked it against my motor. The motor shaft was much smaller than the gear's bore.

  4. Machined an adapter sleeve

    My dad helped me machine the aluminum shaft that came with the differential into a sleeve to fit over the motor shaft. Our drill press isn't very precise, so the hole came out oversized and our heat-fit plan didn't work.

  5. The set screw stripped the sleeve

    We drilled and tapped the side of the sleeve for a grub screw instead. The aluminum walls were too thin to take real clamping force, and the threads stripped as soon as I tightened it.

  6. The motor alignment was off

    I'd positioned the motor for my smaller printed gears. With the larger metal gear, the motor no longer lined up with the differential.

7Biggest lesson

What you order matters. Picking the right parts the first time saves countless hours of work later.

  • Choose parts as a system. The motor, gear, and differential each looked fine alone. The problems came from how they fit together.
  • Design around what the printer can do. Tolerant joints and fasteners beat parts that need perfect accuracy.
  • Keep CAD modular. Separate files made every later fix faster.
  • Double-check dimensions. One radius-for-diameter mistake meant redesigning a part.

Next steps

I'm designing an adapter that either sets onto or fits over the motor shaft, to replace the thin aluminum sleeve that stripped. I'm deciding between ordering it from a machining service like PCBWay or making it from the other end of the shaft I already ordered. After that: getting the Formula RC league going in my engineering club.
